# Cover letter — Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory

Dear Editors,

I submit "Observation-Calibrated Celestial Reference Networks for Long-Horizon Orientation Reconstruction" for consideration as an original research paper in Simulation Modelling Practice and Theory.

This is a simulation paper whose central methodological contribution is a three-layer design that separates theory, observation calibration, and far-horizon extrapolation. The problem: how can Earth's spin-axis direction be encoded at a reference epoch so that it remains reconstructable after 10^3–10^8 years, when every individual reference source will have drifted, faded, been lost, or been misidentified? The deeper question — one the simulation community faces in any deep-future or poorly observed domain — is which model conclusions deserve belief when the parameters that drive them are not directly measurable.

Layer 1 is a general synthetic theory: a controlled parameter-space experiment mapping the recoverability surface P(recover | drift, loss, misidentification, geometry, storage precision, estimator, chirality) for seven reference-network architecture classes. It identifies where reconstruction works, where it fails, and which parameters dominate — a response-surface and failure-boundary study rather than a single-scenario forecast.

Layer 2 is observation-calibrated validation: real Gaia DR3 bright stars (measured five/six-parameter astrometry), ATNF pulsars (measured timing and kinematics, with missingness reported rather than imputed), and ICRF3 extragalactic sources (real anisotropic geometry plus a literature-constrained stability prior) are mapped into the same parameter space, and the identical reconstruction task is re-run on networks built from the observed populations — random versus D-optimally selected, at equal counts so composition effects are isolated. The horizons are explicitly tiered: 10^3–10^5 yr observation-anchored, 10^6 yr extended physically constrained projection, and 10^7–10^8 yr reported separately as extrapolative stress tests, never as calibrated forecasts. The paper's signature section then classifies eight pre-declared synthetic conclusions as confirmed, partially confirmed, reversed, or not testable under observation-based calibration — a worked example of auditing a simulation study against its own assumptions.

Verification and validation follow established simulation V&V categories: unit and identity tests, known-truth recovery, catalogue geometry reproduction, empirical distribution checks, a direct angular-linear versus 3D-Cartesian propagation comparison, quality-cut sensitivity, negative controls, and a four-branch sensitivity grid that bounds every remaining non-empirical parameter explicitly. Results are non-obvious after calibration: heterogeneity is not automatically beneficial — an equal-count observed-population hybrid trails an equal-count ICRF3 network — and the calibration gap between declared scenario parameters and observed populations is itself quantified as a finding.

Reproducibility is integral: the complete pipeline — catalogue snapshots with checksums and acquisition ledgers, both simulation layers, calibration registry and parameter classification, summary statistics, figures, tables, and every manuscript number — is regenerated by a single `make all` from the public repository https://github.com/bougtoir/prospective-archaeoastronomy with a fixed master seed; manuscript values are machine-extracted and audited for traceability. All equations are native Word equation objects.

I believe the paper fits SMPT's interest in modelling methodology with explicit validation, calibration, and reproducibility rather than scenario-specific prediction. I confirm that the manuscript is original and is not under consideration elsewhere. Competing interests, funding, CRediT roles, generative-AI use, and data-availability declarations are included.
